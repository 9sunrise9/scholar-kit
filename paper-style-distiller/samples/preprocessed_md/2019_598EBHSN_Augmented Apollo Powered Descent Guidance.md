I.
Introduction B Y THE time this paper is published, we will be celebrating the 50th anniversary of the lunar landing of Apollo 11. The Apollo powered descent guidance (APDG) technology that landed the Apollo Lunar Module on the moon is one of the many technological innovations of the time that enabled the ultimate success. The APDG law, as flown, assumes a thrust acceleration vector profile of a quadratic function of time [1–3]. Using the current position and velocity vectors as the initial condition, the coefficients of the quadratic polynomial are uniquely determined by meeting the targeted final position, velocity, andthrust acceleration (all vectors) at a specified final time. Another version of the powered guidance law developed at the same time is known as the E-guidance [4], although it was not actually flown. In the E-guidance approach (likely named after the notation to a key matrix in the derivation), the commanded thrust acceleration vector is defined by a linear polynomial of time, and the targeting condition is specified by the final position and velocity vectorsat the prescribed final time. Later, it is discoveredthat the E-guidance law is actually the solution to an optimal landing problem in a constant gravity field with a quadratic performance index on the thrust acceleration [5]. Even though E-guidance is not theoretically fuel-optimal (which requires the performance index to be an integral of the thrust acceleration magnitude, not a quadratic performance index), it typically yields a smaller propellant consumption in the same problem than APDG for the same burn time.
Both APDG and E-guidance laws have simple and elegant closedform solutions. Since the Apollo program, APDG has been the baseline descent guidance approach for numerous planetary landing studies and actual missions [6–10]. Still, APDG and E-guidance represent a technology more than 50 years old. There are aspects of APDG and E-guidance that are becoming the bottleneck for the venerable technology to meet the ever-increasingdemand for mission autonomy and higher system performance. Two such major issues are the determination of the powered descent initiation (PDI) condition and initial time-to-go. The PDI point is where the engine is ignited and the powered descent phase is commenced. Time-to-go is a parameter in the E-guidance and APDG laws that specifies the remaining burn time. Most of the follow-on applications adopt a similar technique for a single-parameter threshold-based PDI trigger outside the powered descent guidance system. The importance of an appropriate PDI condition and the initial value of the time-to-go cannot be overstressed. The PDI condition is highly critical to the success of APDG and E-guidance because it defines the divert requirement, propellant usage, and whether the required thrust magnitude will saturate severely on the maximum or minimum allowable bound (which in turn determines whether the targeting condition can be achieved) [11]. However, a good PDI condition is really a function of the full trajectory state, and its determination cannot be adequately captured by the information on a single parameter. Therefore, a PDI trigger based on the threshold value of one variable/parameter must be sufficiently conservative at the cost of a considerable propellant penalty, or it risks failing to lead to successful landing in significantly off-nominal or dispersed cases.
The value of time-to-go has similar effects. Too short or too long a time-to-go can render the powered descent problem infeasible. Under otherwise the same condition, a shorter (yet still feasible) time-to-go tends to cause a smaller propellant usage. But a most appropriate initial value of time-to-go is really dependent on the PDI condition.
The conventional way to determine the trigger and corresponding time-to-go is through trial and error in simulations on the ground. And they will necessarily be conservative to accommodate dispersed cases. This means that the propellant performance is likely to suffer a substantial penalty.
Motivated by the ongoing need for reliable and high-precision landing in planetary missions (robotic or human in the future) and the renewed interest in human lunar landing missions, a methodology we call Augmented Apollo Powered Descent Guidance (A2 PDG) is developed in this paper to combine the invaluable heritage of APDG and some results of the recent advanced descent guidance research toward a fully autonomous powered descent system. At the front end of A2 PDG is still the flight-proven Apollo powered guidance method, except that a tunable Apollo powered guidance (TAPDG) law reigns. Unlike the classical APDG or E-Guidance law, in which no parameter is left for the user to adjust, the TAPDG law has a guidance gain that can be tuned. The E-guidance law and APDG law turn out to be the special cases of the TAPDG law for certainvalues of that guidance parameter. But for other values of thegain, theguidance law is a new variant that trades off between propellant usage and trajectory shaping. This tunable guidance law is further augmented by the unique capability of A2 PDG of autonomous determination of a favorable PDI condition and corresponding time-to-go. As part of its functionalities, A2 PDG determines the PDI point on the basis of the actual fight condition by a recently developed algorithm dubbed universal powered guidance (UPG) [12]. Onboard Powered Descent Initiation Determination Onboard Powered Descent Initiation Determination An adaptive PDI determination approach is proposed in conjunction with the deployment of UPG in Refs. [12,13] mainly for further reducing propellant usage in a Mars EDL mission. The same logic, with an important caveat to be discussed in the next subsection, remains applicable in planetary EDL applications when a version of the Apollo powered descent guidance law is employed.
Let us state the underlying assumption required for this strategy as follows.
Assumption: Before the powered descent phase, the vehicle is on a ballistic, lifting entry trajectory or any other combinations that are independent of the powered descent trajectory. The only requirement is that this trajectory leads toward the general direction of the landing site, so that the range-to-go to the landing site is decreasing along the trajectory (at least until some point near the end of the trajectory).

Guidance Margin Guarantee It is theoretically established in Ref. [12] that the free-time fueloptimal powered descent solution in a constant gravity field in general has a three-arc bang–bang thrust magnitude profile of Tmax −Tmin −Tmax. See the profile in red solid line in Fig. 4. The burn times of the second and third arcs are optimized by UPG. See Ref. [12] for more details. For a given unpowered trajectory, the onboard PDI logic will enable the powered descent guidance algorithm to use nearly the minimum amount of propellant. However, if the unpowered trajectory changes, the PDI condition will change, hence the associated minimum propellant usage. It is discovered in Refs. Note that the statements of the assumption are intentionally less precise because the specific nature of this trajectory is immaterial, and the trajectory before powered descent need not be subject to a particular accuracy condition.

The onboard PDI logic makes use of the powerful and reliable capability of UPG in finding the fuel-optimal soft-landing solution and the complete associated trajectory information, starting at any point along the incoming trajectory. In particular, the touchdown location along the soft-landing trajectory will be predicted, and the range traveled along the soft-landing trajectory will be known. This predicted touchdown range initially will be shorter than the current ground range-to-go to the landing site. See Fig. 3. As the vehicle continues its flight on the unpowered trajectory, the assumption ensures that the actual range-to-go will be decreasing. Eventually, the predicted soft-landing range will exceed the current range-to-go as the vehicle approaches the landing site on the unpowered flight, as illustrated by the latter soft-landing trajectory in Fig. 3. This is the time when PDI should be commenced.
Recall that the soft-landing trajectory demands no divert maneuver from the vehicle, and the propellant consumption is the least among all powered descent trajectories that land softly on the ground. The assumption also implies that the soft-landing touchdown location from the PDI found in the preceding way will not be unreasonably far from the designated landing site. Thus, the divert required to land at the designated site will be moderate, and the propellant usage will be close to optimal.
Note that, in the PDI logic described previously, the comparison of ground ranges is used. One might be tempted to use the distance between the predicted soft-landing touchdown location and the designated landing site as the trigger for PDI; when this distance becomes minimum, the PDI starts. The difficulty is that the minimum ofthisdistanceisnotknowninadvance.Andthereisevennoguarantee that the variations of this distance are unimodal, that is, it decreases monotonically first, then increases monotonically after it reaches the minimum. Therefore, monitoring this distance and triggering PDI as soon as this distance begins to increase can end up starting PDI prematurely, as we have observed in our simulations. In contrast, checking the sign of the difference between the predicted soft-landing touchdown range and actual range-to-go is not predicated on any particular assumed variation pattern, and this sign is assured to change as the vehicle continues to fly toward the landing site.

In other words, we have a convenient and physically intuitive “knob” in the form of constraint (43) to tune in the A2 PDG method for the classical tradeoff between robustness and performance.
A typical value of Δ2 that suffices for the applications in this paper is 2–5 s.

Conclusions In aerospace guidance and control, especially for space flight, great premium is placed on technologies that have been flown before.
The venerable Apollo powered descent guidance (APDG) approach is such a technology that landed the Lunar Module on the moon for all successful lunar landing missions. Yet, the needs 50 years later for autonomous operations, higher performance, and diverse applications have amplified some of the weaknesses in the APDG. In this work, a methodology called Augmented Apollo Powered Descent Guidance (A2 PDG) isdevelopedto modernize the legacytechnology of APDG with the latest research advances.A family of variants of the Apollo powered descent guidance law are derived from a perspective of implicit guidance. The classical APDG law and well-known Apollo E-guidance law are shown to be two special cases of this family. Any member of the tunable guidance laws is defined by the value of an adjustable gain and shares the similar simplicity, reliability, and predictability of the APDG approach. At the front end of A2 PDG, the guidance command is generated by such a tunable Apollo powered descent guidance law. Behind the scenes, the guidance system is supported by a novel online powered descent initiation (PDI) logic based on an advanced propellant-optimal rocket descent algorithm. This addition addresses effectively the longstanding challenge of determining online a good PDI condition and initial time-to-go when applying APDG, and it allows the guidance system to provide a nearly fuel-optimal performance. The latter (fueloptimality) is critically important for a high-mass Mars mission. The A2 PDG framework inherits the strengths of the flight-proven legacy technology of APDG yet enables the powered descent guidance system to be fully autonomous, propellant-efficient, and adaptive to different flight conditions and even in-flight change of landing site.
Extensive end-to-end closed-loop Monte Carlo simulations for a human Mars mission have provided compelling evidence to the potential of the A2 PDG methodology.
